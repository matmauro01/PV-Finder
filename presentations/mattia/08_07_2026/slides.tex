\begin{frame}{Motivation}
    \begin{columns}[c]
        \begin{column}{0.52\linewidth}
            \textbf{Primary-vertex finding} reconstructs the proton--proton
            interaction points along the beam axis from charged-particle tracks.
            \readingonly{It underlies essentially every LHC measurement.}

            \vspace{0.55em}
            \textbf{PV-Finder} is a neural network that maps tracks directly to a
            1-D histogram of vertex density, then extracts positions by peak finding.

            \vspace{0.55em}
            \textbf{Key advantage:} the current default, the Adaptive Multi-Vertex
            Finder (\textbf{AMVF}), is iterative: its runtime grows with the number of
            vertices (${\sim}200$\,ms/event at $\mu{=}90$). PV-Finder evaluates a
            fixed-size map, so its inference is \textbf{flat with pileup}, critical for
            Run 3 ($\langle\mu\rangle\!\approx\!60$) and HL-LHC ($\langle\mu\rangle\!\approx\!200$).
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.48\linewidth}
            \begin{figure}
                \centering
                \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/timing_1.png}
            \end{figure}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Resolution Metric: \(\sigma_{\text{vtx-vtx}}\)}
    \begin{columns}[c]

\begin{frame}{TTVA: A Small Graph Attention Network}
    \begin{columns}[c]

\begin{frame}{Backup: Where the Peak-Finder Thresholds Come From}
    \label{bk:thresholds}
    \begin{columns}[c]
        \begin{column}{0.46\linewidth}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=\linewidth, keepaspectratio]{images/height_floor_tradeoff.png}\\[2pt]
            {\scriptsize\textcolor{black!55}{Fakes per event (red) and efficiency (blue)
            against the height floor. The deployed value is $0.03$.}}
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.54\linewidth}
            \footnotesize
            \textbf{What we run.} Three cuts on every candidate peak: at least
            $\mathbf{3}$ \textbf{bins wide}, \textbf{integral} $\mathbf{\geq 0.30}$,
            \textbf{height} $\mathbf{\geq 0.03}$.
            \vspace{0.55em}

            \textbf{How we picked it.} Split the held-out sample in half with a fixed
            seed, scan a $120$-point grid over the three cuts on \textbf{half A}, then
            report the winner on \textbf{half B}, which was never used to choose
            anything. Half B gives $87.0\%$ efficiency at $16.2$ fakes/event.
            \vspace{0.55em}

            \textbf{Why not add a classifier on top?} A boosted tree does tell real
            peaks from fake ones, \textbf{AUC 0.965}, and histogram features alone do as
            well as track features, so the network has already used the tracks.
            \textcolor{pennred}{\textbf{But separable is not the same as filterable.}}
            These three cuts have already removed the easy fakes. What survives sits
            where the two populations overlap, so any further filter takes real peaks
            along with it.
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}
    \vspace{0.3em}
    \centering
    {\small\textcolor{black!65}{\textbf{817 further filters were tried and scored the
    same way. None beat these three cuts.} \textcolor{black!50}{(backup)}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup: Why the Split Rule Is Not Smarter}
    \small
    The rule splits a run as soon as the histogram starts rising again, even by one
    bin. The obvious fix is a \textbf{prominence gate}: split only if the valley between
    the two peaks is deep enough.
    \vspace{0.5em}

    \begin{columns}[t]
        \begin{column}{0.42\linewidth}
            \centering
            \scriptsize
            \setlength{\tabcolsep}{5pt}
            \begin{tabular}{l rr}
                \toprule
                gate & fakes cut & real lost \\
                \midrule
                none \textcolor{black!55}{(deployed)} & 0 & 0 \\
                $0.005$ & 3.37 & 1.70 \\
                $0.010$ & 4.58 & 2.07 \\
                $0.020$ & 6.85 & 2.76 \\
                \bottomrule
            \end{tabular}\\[0.3em]
            {\scriptsize\textcolor{black!55}{peaks per event}}
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.58\linewidth}
            \footnotesize
            \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.35em}
                \item \textbf{The arithmetic fails.} One real peak lost per event costs
                    $0.90$ efficiency points, one fake removed is worth $0.20$. A filter
                    needs \textbf{$4.5$ fakes per real peak}; these manage $1.3$ to
                    $2.0$
                \item \textbf{It breaks what the rule is for.}
                    $\sigma_{\text{vtx-vtx}}$ degrades $0.214 \to 0.246$\,mm: it also
                    removes peaks that were correctly resolving close pairs
                \item \textbf{Nothing is left to catch.} Surviving surplus peaks have
                    median prominence $0.068$, and $0.89$ relative to their own height:
                    standalone low peaks, not ripple
            \end{itemize}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}

    \vspace{0.4em}
    \centering
    {\footnotesize\textcolor{black!65}{\textbf{817 configurations} were swept in all,
    chosen on one half of the sample and scored on the other.
    \textbf{None beat what we run.}}}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup: Why Not a Looser Matching Window}
    \vfill
    \small
    A round number like $0.5$\,mm looks safely generous. It is not. At PU200 there are
    about $0.9$ truth vertices per mm, so a reconstructed vertex lands near \emph{some}
    truth vertex whether or not it found anything.
    \vspace{0.6em}

    \centering
    To measure that, displace the whole reconstructed list by $3$\,mm, destroying every
    real association, and match again. Whatever survives is coincidence:
    \vspace{0.6em}

    \footnotesize
    \setlength{\tabcolsep}{9pt}
    \begin{tabular}{l rrrr}
        \toprule
        window \textcolor{black!55}{[mm]} & 0.10 & \textbf{0.17} & 0.50 & 1.00 \\
        \midrule
        efficiency from coincidence \textcolor{black!55}{[\%]}
            & 11.1 & \textbf{18.6} & \textcolor{pennred}{\textbf{45.4}} & 68.1 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}

    \vspace{0.8em}
    \raggedright\small
    At $0.5$\,mm nearly \textbf{half} the measured efficiency is coincidence; at
    $0.17$\,mm it is under a fifth. The floor is the same for both algorithms to within
    $0.3$ points, so it does not bias the \emph{comparison}, but it does destroy the
    meaning of any \emph{absolute} efficiency quoted at a loose window.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup: The Train-on-Truth, Deploy-on-Peaks Gap}
    \small
    \begin{columns}[t]
        \begin{column}{0.52\linewidth}
            We train the associator on truth vertices and deploy it on peaks, so the
            vertex nodes differ at deployment:
            \begin{itemize}
                \setlength\itemsep{0.35em}
                \item \textbf{Bookkeeping}: empty vertices used to be booked Fake,
                    fixed by the drop-empty convention
                \item \textbf{Distribution shift}: peak positions, widths and heights
                    differ from truth. On the current sample \textbf{13.9\%} of peaks
                    have no $\ntrk\geq2$ truth vertex within $0.5$\,mm, against a
                    \textbf{9.1\%} floor from truth association, and \textbf{21.9\%} of truth vertices
                    have no peak at all
            \end{itemize}
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.48\linewidth}
            \textbf{Chain fakes are the finder's, not the associator's.} The difference
            between the $13.9\%$ junk rate and the $9.1\%$ floor from truth association is a
            truth-convention artifact: a peak sitting on a real single-track interaction
            has truth \emph{tracks} but no eligible truth \emph{vertex}.
            \vspace{0.6em}

            \vspace{0.2em}
            \textbf{The obvious fix does not work.} Resampling the training vertex nodes
            to look like reconstructed peaks was expected to close the gap. The
            \emph{unaugmented} control wins at every threshold, $0.684$ against $0.650$,
            and the checkpoint-to-checkpoint spread is only $0.002$, so the effect is
            $16\times$ the epoch noise.
            \vspace{0.5em}

            {\footnotesize\textcolor{black!55}{Caveat: 6 dataset passes against the
            earlier campaign's 18, so this says augmentation does not help at equal
            training budget, not that it never helps.}}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup: The GBT Fake Classifier}
    \begin{columns}[c]
        \begin{column}{0.5\linewidth}
            \small
            \textbf{Setup:} a gradient-boosted-tree classifier scores each peak real vs
            fake (real $=$ within $0.5$\,mm of a truth vertex), from \textbf{23 per-peak features}:
            \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.2em}
                \item track counts and quality in windows around the peak
                \item histogram shape (height, local integral, FWHM, curvature)
                \item neighborhood (distance and ratio to the nearest peak)
            \end{itemize}
            \vspace{0.4em}
            Trained on one PU200 run, evaluated on an \textbf{independent} run with no
            event overlap.
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.5\linewidth}
            \small
            \textbf{Ablation (AUC):} all 23 $=0.965$, histogram-only $=0.959$,
            track-only $=0.957$. The two groups are \textbf{largely redundant}; the
            single most important feature is the \textbf{local integral} within
            $\pm0.5$\,mm, importance $0.72$.
            \vspace{0.5em}

            \textbf{The gate does not transfer between models.} On the model it was
            trained for, threshold $0.3$ removed ${\sim}38\%$ of fakes at $0.9$ points
            of efficiency. On the current model the same threshold removes
            \textbf{$7.4\%$}. Discrimination survives (AUC $0.949$), but the
            \textbf{calibration} moved by a factor of five.
            \vspace{0.5em}

            {\footnotesize\textcolor{black!55}{The discriminating signal does live in the
            histogram, which is the argument for an in-model head that cleans the
            histogram at training time rather than a post-hoc gate.}}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup: Loss Re-weighting, a Negative Result}
    \begin{columns}[c]
        \begin{column}{0.55\linewidth}
            \small
            \begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0.4em}
                \item The target peak height rises steeply with \ntrk, so under MSE
                    \textbf{missing a tall peak costs $50\times$ more} than missing a
                    short one, and the misses live at low \ntrk
                \item Tested a weighted MSE that flattens the ratio to $8\times$, three
                    arms trained in parallel
                \item At \textbf{matched fake rate} the arms are
                    \textbf{indistinguishable}, and plain MSE has the best resolution
            \end{itemize}
        \end{column}
        \begin{column}{0.45\linewidth}
            \small
            \textbf{Verdict: keep plain MSE.}
            \vspace{0.5em}

            The apparent gain visible on the training monitor was an amplitude-scale
            artifact, not a change in the vertices found.
            \vspace{0.5em}

            {\footnotesize\textcolor{black!55}{This is why the remaining fake-suppression
            ideas are aimed at the \emph{target definition} and at an explicit
            objectness output, rather than at re-weighting the same target.}}
        \end{column}
    \end{columns}
\end{frame}
